## Product requirement, and the pattern underneath S1 and S9

### The requirement, now stated

Recorded here because the corpus has never contained it, and three findings depend on it. It is a **new requirement, not a missed one** — it was articulated on 26 Sep, after the handoff, so no prior review or implementation work could have accounted for it:

> **The Desk runs itself.** Human involvement is exactly two things: (a) a **toggleable pre-publish review** — roughly ten minutes per episode, on at launch, switched off once volume grows or the pipeline has earned confidence; and (b) **exception handling** — errors, typed halts, and events that genuinely require supervision. Take selection is not a review point. Acoustic judgement per block is not a review point.

Until now, **unattended operation was never declared as a product requirement anywhere in the corpus.** "Unattended" appears six times across ~12,700 lines — every one about publishing mechanics, assembly recovery, or cost at scale. Never about editorial or acoustic autonomy. That omission is the root of S1 and the reason S9 has no socket.

### The asymmetry

The editorial half of the pipeline is designed to run unattended. The audio half reads as though an operator is standing at the console.

Upstream — planning, writing, audit — every blocking path has an automatic primary route with a human as the *override*. Claims Policy §23: resolution requires "a revision that removes/corrects the problem plus a clean re-audit, **or** a named human adjudication where the gate class permits it." Architecture bounds that loop with per-stage revision budgets and a typed `revision_limit_exceeded` halt. Writing §21 makes the reviser the writer model. That is a complete unattended loop: fail → revise → re-audit → N times → typed halt.

Downstream — takes, rerolls, acoustic quality — the human sits on the *normal* path instead. This is **not** inherited from the prototype: that ran in Python on GitHub and published straight through to R2 and RSS with nobody in the loop. It is two drafting artifacts:

1. **Engineering Standards §4 is an authorization list**, not a workflow rule. It describes how privileged actions must be *represented* — with actor identity. Take selection got grouped with three genuinely-human actions (overrides, correction adjudication, publication) and inherited "operator-only" by association.
2. **P&R §49's "not 'try again until it sounds nice'"** is an anti-pattern warning against unbounded regeneration chasing acoustic taste. The warning is sound. It reads as a workflow requirement.

Neither is a decision anyone made. With the requirement above stated, both resolve toward automation — and the genuine QC need lands somewhere else entirely, which is S9.

### S9 design notes

Three things worth settling when the gate is actually built, none of which needs building now:

**Where the hold goes.** Between `READY` and `REVALIDATED`, not after it. Revalidation is the cheap automated check for "has the world changed since freeze" and it carries a short expiry TTL — so it should be the *last* thing before publish, running fresh **after** approval rather than before it. That ordering means a human sign-off can't go stale in a dangerous way: if a result is corrected or a serious incident is confirmed between approval and publish, revalidation still catches it and forces the rebuild. Episode ID, GUID and pubDate are already minted at entry to `READY`, so the thing under review has stable identity.

**The review window is bounded by freshness, not patience.** Architecture defines a preferred freshness horizon (~36h) and a configured hard horizon. An episode parked awaiting review is burning that budget. Past the hard horizon, publication is deterministically blocked and the episode must be rebuilt — so a forgotten review doesn't just delay an episode, it destroys it. Ten minutes per show is trivially affordable; forgetting until Tuesday is not. The hold needs a timeout policy, and the honest default is **expire to halt, not expire to publish**.

**What ten minutes can actually cover.** One listen of a ~9-minute master, plus a glance at the audit findings and the claim list. That is a whole-episode unit, which is why this belongs at `READY` and not at take selection — nobody reviews seven render blocks in ten minutes. Architecture's operator view already specifies most of what the screen would need.

What a human ear catches in that window is **acoustic and tonal**, not factual: wrong voice identity, a mangled club or player name, the model reading a stage direction aloud, an overlap collapsing into two sequential lines, and — the case that actually earns the gate — an episode that is technically compliant but tonally wrong for the week. That is precisely P&R §30's "soft/manual" list, which no automated check covers. It is *not* a fact-check: nobody verifies a claim against its evidence by ear in ten minutes. Factual integrity remains the job of the deterministic gates and the semantic audit, and the review must not be treated as a safety net under them.

**Launch scope, and the off-switch.** The gate is a launch-phase measure at 1–3 entities, not a permanent stage. Two notes for whoever builds it:

- **Concurrency is the real constraint, not the freshness clock.** With three clubs on a normal Saturday, three matches can finish within an hour of each other, three pipelines complete within minutes of each other, and three episodes park simultaneously. That is thirty minutes of concentrated attention at a fixed and unpredictable time — a very different thing from "ten minutes per show." So the hold must handle **N parked episodes at once** and let them be worked serially; it cannot assume one at a time. It also argues for a generous timeout even when the intent is to review immediately, and for enough slack in each show's publication slot to absorb a serial queue.
- **Make the off-switch evidence-based.** "Once I'm confident" is not a trigger anyone can act on later. Record each review outcome — approved unchanged / halted / rebuilt — and retire the gate when approved-unchanged has held for N consecutive episodes. That also reveals *what* the gate is catching, which is the list of things worth automating next. Architecture already treats repeated overrides as calibration data; this is the same principle inverted.

## What may already be built wrong

Two flags for an agent mid-implementation:

**⚠ S1 — check for an approval queue at the take level.** If the render slice has started and any take-selection UI, queue, pending state, or "awaiting operator" state has appeared *per render block*, remove it: takes auto-approve on technical validation. Note the distinction from S9 — a durable suspend capability in the **workflow engine** is wanted, and belongs once at `READY` for the whole episode. Seven pauses per episode at the block level is the thing to avoid. If the render slice hasn't started, S1 costs nothing.

**⚠ S2 — check what the agent has read.** If it has consulted Lore Pipeline v0.1 or Ingest & Digest v0.1 for any schema decision — most likely pronunciation, source retention, or anything touching sombre/incident state — those decisions need re-deriving from Architecture v1.0, Claims Policy v0.1, and Performance & Render v0.1. Worth asking the agent directly which documents it has used; it should be able to say.

Lower priority, but if the brief or script schema is already written, S4's version fields and S6's two brief fields are cheaper to add now than after rows exist.

Unchanged: **`READY FOR BUILD 1 AFTER SPECIFIC BLOCKERS`**.

The residual risk in this project is not architectural — it is that good documents are sitting next to obsolete ones without labels, and that the one artifact meant to demonstrate all of it was frozen before anyone checked it.
